## Detailed Table Caption

Table 2 reports subgroup performance disparities across sex, age, and race demographics for all models with available fairness analysis from Task 6. Gap is the max–min difference across subgroups within the corresponding demographic attribute (e.g., for sex, gap = |AUROC_Female − AUROC_Male|; for age, gap = max(AUROC_<40, AUROC_40-65, AUROC_65+) − min(...); for race, gap = max(AUROC_Asian, AUROC_Black, AUROC_Hispanic, AUROC_White) − min(...)). Smaller gap values indicate smaller disparities and hence better subgroup parity. Both AUROC gaps (primary metric) and AUPRC gaps (secondary metric) are reported. Overall AUROC and AUPRC values in this table are sourced from Task 6 fairness analysis (where subgroup gaps were computed) to ensure metric consistency within each row; these values may differ slightly from Table 1 (which reports aggregated results from Task 4 and Task 5) due to differences in preprocessing, data definitions, or random seeds used in the fairness pipeline. Seven models have complete fairness outputs from Task 6: LSTM, Standard Transformer, ChronoFormer, PatchTST, and three TGT variants (Temporal-only, Clinical-only, Full). Three models (Logistic Regression, LightGBM, Pretrained TST) lack fairness analysis in Task 6 and are marked NA. The table tests the hypothesis that explicit graph-based architectures improve demographic fairness relative to sequential and attention-based baselines.

## Results

Table 2 reports subgroup performance disparities (fairness gaps) for LSTM, Standard Transformer, ChronoFormer, PatchTST, and three TGT graph-based variants across sex, age, and race demographics. Across all fairness metrics (six total: sex AUROC gap, age AUROC gap, race AUROC gap, sex AUPRC gap, age AUPRC gap, race AUPRC gap), the graph-based models did not demonstrate a consistent subgroup-parity advantage over non-graph baselines. Instead, performance disparities were generally comparable in magnitude—and often larger—than those observed in sequential and attention-based models. To evaluate the core architectural hypothesis, we examine LSTM (standard sequential baseline) as the comparative standard against TGT Full (combined temporal and clinical graph features). In this controlled comparison, LSTM achieved smaller performance gaps on five of six fairness metrics: sex AUROC gap (LSTM 0.0026 vs TGT Full 0.0098), age AUROC gap (LSTM 0.0640 vs TGT Full 0.0709), race AUROC gap (LSTM 0.1820 vs TGT Full 0.2259), age AUPRC gap (LSTM 0.0971 vs TGT Full 0.1379), and race AUPRC gap (LSTM 0.1939 vs TGT Full 0.2677). TGT Full showed an advantage only on sex AUPRC gap (0.0157 vs LSTM 0.0233), which does not offset its substantially larger racial disparities on AUROC. A secondary comparison between TGT Clinical-only and Standard Transformer (a non-graph attention baseline) yielded mixed results: Standard Transformer delivered better performance gaps on AUROC metrics across all three demographics (sex: 0.0068 vs 0.0094, age: 0.0554 vs 0.0639, race: 0.1761 vs 0.1774), while TGT Clinical-only showed advantages only on age and sex AUPRC gaps. Across the full set of models, no single architecture family dominated fairness; different demographic attributes exhibited best-in-class performance in different models (e.g., TGT Temporal-only achieved the smallest sex AUROC gap at 0.0023, while Standard Transformer achieved the smallest age and race AUROC gaps at 0.0554 and 0.1761 respectively). These heterogeneous patterns—where sequential models outperform graph-based on AUROC parity, graph-based show mixed AUPRC results, and non-graph attention models remain competitive—suggest that demographic fairness is not strongly correlated with graph-based architecture alone. Instead, fairness outcomes appear influenced by a combination of architectural components, training objectives, and possibly model capacity and regularization effects that are independent of explicit graph structure. These findings indicate that while graph-based models offer potential advantages for capturing temporal and multi-modal clinical dependencies (as explored in Table 1), they do not inherently solve or substantially improve the fairness–accuracy tradeoff across patient demographic groups in this ICU mortality prediction task.
